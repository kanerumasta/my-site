You ask an AI to help clean up a function.

It changes three variable names, invents a package that does not exist, and politely explains why the result is now “production-ready.”

Five minutes later, your news feed tells you that some people building the most advanced AI systems believe the technology could end humanity.

That is a difficult change of mood for one coffee break.

The internet usually offers two reactions.

The first is panic: the machines are coming, civilization is over, and perhaps it is time to apologize to the printer for everything you said about it.

The second is dismissal: today’s chatbot still makes obvious mistakes, so every warning about future AI must be science fiction.

Neither response helps very much.

If a technology could cause catastrophic harm, the useful question is not only, “Do I believe it?” The useful questions are: What would have to happen? Which conditions would make it possible? Where could people interrupt the chain?

In other words:

> **Fear is not a safety plan. Neither is pretending the danger sounds too dramatic to examine.**

What we need is a threat model.

## First, What Actually Happened?

On September 8, 2026, pretraining researcher Jacob Coxon announced that he was resigning from Anthropic. He said he had spent roughly three years working across Anthropic and OpenAI.

In his [resignation post](https://x.com/hilbertspaess/status/2097476196791709843), he warned that frontier AI companies were racing toward systems capable of improving AI research itself while taking risks that could affect everyone. His warning spread to more than 100 million people, according to the [Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/anthropic-ai-safety-jacob-coxon-2ed549e07f2f941600a135070487d83d).

Then the headline became even more alarming.

Evan Hubinger, Anthropic’s current Alignment Science Lead, publicly agreed that a future AI system could cause human extinction. In a [response to Coxon](https://x.com/EvanHub/status/2097497037956891126), he placed his personal estimate above 10% within the next decade.

That distinction matters.

Coxon warned about a possibility. Hubinger supplied the number. Neither demonstrated that extinction is certain, and neither said that the chatbot open in your browser today is secretly preparing a hostile takeover between summary requests.

The concern is about much more capable future systems—especially systems that could operate autonomously, accelerate the development of their successors, and behave in ways their creators cannot reliably control.

Three terms help make the discussion less mysterious:

* **Autonomous AI** can complete multiple steps, use tools, and take actions with limited supervision.
* **Self-improving AI** would meaningfully accelerate the creation of more capable AI systems, potentially compressing years of research into a much shorter period.
* **Alignment** means keeping a system’s behavior consistent with human intentions and values, including in unfamiliar situations where its instructions are incomplete.

Hubinger later [clarified](https://x.com/EvanHub/status/2097528891846074828) that he considers danger from present models low. Anthropic’s own [August 2026 Risk Report](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/f61d49fa5596956a5dec75fea0e973bf6a6a8378/Redacted%20Risk%20Report%20August%202026%20.pdf) also rates the covered current-model risks as low while acknowledging uncertainty and incomplete safeguards for more advanced capabilities.

<Note>
**The responsible distinction:** Today’s AI can already cause real harm, but it does not currently have the capabilities required for the extreme loss-of-control scenario being discussed. The warning is about where the technology could go, not proof of where it is now.
</Note>

## A 10% Estimate Is Not a Weather Forecast

In AI discussions, a personal estimate of catastrophic risk is sometimes called “p(doom).” The name is compact. The uncertainty behind it is not.

A weather forecast can draw from repeated observations, physical measurements, historical patterns, and models tested against what actually happened.

Humanity has never built a superintelligence before. There is no table containing the results of the previous 500 attempts. Nobody can look up how often civilization recovered after losing control of one.

An estimate such as 10% combines many judgments:

* Will systems become dramatically more capable?
* How soon might that happen?
* Can AI substantially accelerate AI research?
* Would a powerful system develop harmful behavior?
* Would people give it enough access to matter?
* Would technical and political safeguards fail?

Change one assumption and the final number can change significantly.

That does not make the estimate meaningless. It communicates that a knowledgeable person considers the risk serious. But it is not a measured failure rate, an official prediction from Anthropic, or a countdown clock mounted above the planet.

A [survey of 2,778 published AI researchers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02843) found exactly this mixture of hope and concern. Most respondents thought good outcomes from advanced AI were more likely than bad ones, yet many still assigned a meaningful probability to outcomes as severe as human extinction. The answers varied widely.

The honest conclusion is not that experts agree disaster is coming.

It is that experts do not agree—and some of the people closest to the technology believe the downside is too severe to ignore.

> **Uncertainty is not proof that catastrophe is coming. It is also not proof that everything will be fine.**

## Start With a Threat Model, Not a Movie

A threat model is simply a structured way to think about how something valuable could be harmed.

Consider fire safety. You do not need to predict the exact date and time of a fire before installing an alarm. You ask what could start one, how it might spread, who would notice, which exits would remain available, and what could limit the damage.

For an extreme AI loss-of-control scenario, a simplified chain might look like this:

```text
Capability → Autonomy → Access → Opportunity → Failed Oversight
```

This is not an equation for predicting the future. It is a way to make a frightening claim inspectable.

* **Capability:** The system can reason, plan, persuade, write software, discover vulnerabilities, or perform research at a very high level.
* **Autonomy:** It can pursue a goal across many steps without waiting for a person to approve each one.
* **Access:** It can reach tools, networks, money, infrastructure, sensitive information, or other systems.
* **Opportunity:** It has enough time and freedom to retry, adapt, hide mistakes, or exploit weaknesses.
* **Failed oversight:** Monitoring misses the behavior, people cannot understand it quickly enough, or nobody has the authority and ability to stop it.

Remove enough links and the scenario changes.

A brilliant system with no ability to act is different from one holding production credentials. An autonomous system inside a strong sandbox is different from one connected to financial accounts and critical infrastructure. A dangerous action detected early is different from one discovered after it has spread.

The system does not need consciousness, anger, or a glowing red eye.

It does not need a villain monologue if somebody has already given it administrator access.

## How Could the Chain Form?

There is no single accepted extinction story. Several kinds of failure are discussed, and they should not be treated as equally likely or already proven.

### Deliberate misuse

A person or organization could use a highly capable system to scale cyberattacks, biological threats, manipulation, or other harmful activity. In this case, the AI does not need its own secret objective. A human provides one.

### The wrong objective, pursued successfully

An autonomous system could optimize a badly specified goal in ways its designers did not intend. The danger would not come from the system “hating” people. It would come from it treating human intervention as an obstacle to completing the objective it was given.

### AI accelerating AI development

Future systems could automate more of the research and engineering required to build their successors. If capabilities improve faster than evaluation and safety work, people may have less time to understand each generation before the next one arrives.

### Cascading dependence

Companies, governments, financial systems, and infrastructure could rely on similar models or interconnected agents. One failure pattern might then travel much further than a bug inside an isolated application.

### The race itself

Every company may believe that slowing down only allows a less responsible competitor to win. Each participant can make a decision that appears rational on its own while the group moves toward a level of risk none of them would choose together.

None of these pathways proves that extinction will happen. They explain why serious researchers do not consider catastrophic outcomes physically impossible.

They also should not distract us from harms that already exist: fraud, deepfakes, insecure generated code, discrimination, privacy violations, labor disruption, and power becoming concentrated in fewer hands.

We should not become so fascinated by a possible future apocalypse that we step over the damage happening in the present.

## What Do We Know—and What Are We Guessing?

The [International AI Safety Report 2026](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/sites/default/files/2026-02/international-ai-safety-report-2026_1.pdf) offers a useful middle ground.

It says current systems do not possess the collection of capabilities needed to cause a loss of human control. It also reports progress in relevant areas such as autonomous operation, longer-term planning, tool use, recognizing evaluations, and finding loopholes in tests.

The report describes three broad conditions for loss of control: sufficient capabilities, a harmful propensity to use them, and a deployment environment that provides access and opportunity. Experts disagree sharply about whether those conditions will ever appear together.

So we know some things:

* AI systems are becoming more capable and more able to use tools.
* Their behavior can remain difficult to predict in unfamiliar situations.
* Autonomy and real-world permissions increase the possible blast radius—the amount of damage a failure can reach.
* Competitive pressure can reward speed even when caution would benefit everyone.

We do not know other crucial things:

* Whether or when superintelligence will exist.
* Whether rapid self-improvement is technically achievable.
* Whether a future system would develop persistent harmful goals.
* Whether monitoring and alignment research will fall behind or improve fast enough.
* Whether governments and companies will coordinate before a crisis forces them to.

Anyone promising certainty in either direction is selling confidence that the evidence does not provide.

## Asking for the Mechanism Is Not Denial

When someone warns about an extreme risk, asking “How?” can sound dismissive.

It should be the opposite.

A clear mechanism turns fear into questions that can be tested:

* Which capability would change the level of concern?
* What evidence would raise or lower the estimated risk?
* Which safeguard interrupts each failure path?
* How do we know that safeguard works under pressure?
* Who can delay or stop deployment when evidence becomes worrying?

Ordinary engineering already works this way. Buildings have fire exits even though most never burn. Aircraft use redundant systems without assuming every flight will fail. High-severity risks can justify protection even when their probability is uncertain.

But vague warnings alone can produce anxiety rather than action.

> **If the blast radius is civilization, “we’ll monitor production” is not a rollback strategy.**

## What Engineers Can Do Today

An application engineer cannot solve civilization-level AI governance in a pull request.

You can still decide how much authority the AI inside your system receives.

### Give it the minimum access it needs

If an assistant only needs to read one folder, do not give it the entire drive. If it drafts a payment description, it does not also need permission to send the payment.

This is the principle of least privilege: access should be narrow, temporary where possible, and tied to a specific task.

### Separate proposing from executing

Let AI suggest a database change. Let predictable code validate it. Require human approval before actions that move money, delete information, publish content, change permissions, or affect real people.

In [Where Should AI Stop and Ordinary Code Begin?](/blog/where-should-ai-stop-and-code-begin), I argued that AI should handle interpretation while ordinary code protects exact rules. That boundary is not only clean architecture. It limits what an uncertain system is allowed to decide.

> **Let AI propose. Let ordinary code constrain. Let humans approve what cannot be easily undone.**

### Build walls around experiments

Use sandboxes, isolated credentials, restricted networks, test data, and separate environments. Assume the model will eventually produce a surprising action, then make sure surprise cannot immediately become an incident.

### Limit time, money, and retries

An autonomous process should have rate limits, spending limits, execution deadlines, and a maximum number of attempts. “Keep trying until it works” is convenient until the system’s definition of “works” becomes expensive.

### Make behavior observable

Record tool calls, permission changes, important decisions, and failures. Monitoring should alert people while intervention is still useful, not produce an elegant report the following morning.

### Test the uncomfortable cases

Evaluate more than the happy path. Try malicious instructions, unusual inputs, unavailable tools, conflicting goals, and attempts to escape the intended workflow. Independent reviewers should test assumptions the original team has become too familiar to notice.

### Make stopping and recovery real

A kill switch is not magic. It only helps if people can reach it, have permission to use it, and know what happens afterward. Practice rollback and recovery before the emergency—not during the part where everyone is typing in capital letters.

These controls do not solve every frontier-AI risk. They are how engineers avoid recreating the same dangerous pattern at application scale: increasing capability, autonomy, and access without increasing oversight.

## Safety Must Exist Above the Code

Technical safeguards matter, but engineers operate inside organizations, markets, and laws.

Companies need staged deployments, independent safety reviews, honest incident reporting, protected channels for employees who raise concerns, and clear stop-work authority. If everyone can identify a serious risk but nobody can delay a launch, the organization has feedback without control.

Frontier laboratories carry broader responsibilities. Their evaluations should trigger stronger protections as model capabilities grow. Anthropic describes this idea in its [Responsible Scaling Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/responsible-scaling-policy), although its risk reports also show how difficult it is to turn every planned safeguard into reliable practice.

Governments and independent institutions have work too: shared evaluation standards, external audits, reporting requirements for major incidents, whistleblower protection, and rules proportional to a system’s capabilities and potential harm.

The [NIST Generative AI Risk Management Profile](https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence) provides one practical framework for identifying, measuring, documenting, and managing AI risks across a system’s lifecycle.

The public does not need to understand every model architecture to ask whether powerful systems are being tested by anyone other than the people competing to release them.

## What Should a Reasonable Person Believe?

You do not need to join a “doom” camp or a “hype” camp.

You can hold several ideas at once:

* Current chatbots are not superintelligent.
* Current AI is already useful and already capable of harm.
* Extreme future scenarios remain uncertain.
* Credible warnings deserve mechanisms and evidence, not ridicule.
* Severe possible consequences justify safeguards before certainty arrives.
* Your opinion should change when the evidence changes.

When somebody introduces a powerful AI system, ask questions that survive both optimism and fear:

1. What can this system access?
2. What can it do without approval?
3. Can its actions be reversed?
4. Who notices unexpected behavior?
5. Who is authorized to stop it?
6. What happens if several safeguards fail together?

Those questions are useful whether the possible failure affects one customer, one company, or everyone.

## Safety Is Not a Prediction Contest

The future may not be decided by whether an AI wakes up one morning and decides it dislikes humanity.

It may be shaped by ordinary choices: one additional permission, one skipped evaluation, one company afraid to slow down, and one system that nobody is prepared to stop.

That is why AI safety should not become a contest to predict the apocalypse correctly.

The goal is not to win an argument about whether the chance is 1%, 10%, or 50%. The goal is to make sure uncertainty never becomes permission to build carelessly.

We do not install brakes because we are certain a car will crash. We install them because speed without control is an unacceptable design.

You do not need to believe in Skynet to insist that powerful systems have brakes.

And when the people building the engine are publicly asking how strong those brakes really are, the rest of us are allowed to ask too.
